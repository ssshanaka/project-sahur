# Module 7 — Fine-Tuning
## Lesson 7.8 — Epochs (Duration of Training)

We have configured the physical shape of the adapter (Rank, Alpha, Dropout), the optimizer's step size (`learning_rate`), and how much data it looks at per step (`batch_size`). 

The final crucial variable governing the training loop is: **When do we stop?**

This is controlled by `num_train_epochs` or `max_steps`.

Knowing exactly when to stop training is the difference between producing a highly generalized, intelligent assistant, and a broken, overfitted robotic parrot. In this lesson, we will explore the mathematics of training duration, how to mathematically detect when learning turns into memorization, and how to automate the stopping process.

---

## 1. Concept & Theory: The Duration of Learning

First, we must define our terms mathematically.

**1 Step:** A single forward pass, backward pass, and weight update for a single batch of data. 
*(If `batch_size = 2`, one step means the model looked at 2 conversations and updated its weights).*

**1 Epoch:** The model has seen *every single example* in the training dataset exactly once.

The relationship between them is purely mathematical. If your dataset contains 1,000 conversational examples, and your `batch_size` is 2, how many steps does it take to complete 1 epoch?

$$ \text{Steps per Epoch} = \frac{\text{Dataset Size}}{\text{Batch Size}} $$
$$ \text{Steps per Epoch} = \frac{1000}{2} = 500 \text{ steps} $$

### 1.1 The Epoch vs Steps Decision Matrix

When configuring the `SFTTrainer`, you must choose either `num_train_epochs` or `max_steps`. Which should you use? Here is the formal decision matrix based on dataset size, stability, and cloud budget:

| Scenario | Recommended Parameter | Why? |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Massive pre-training** (100M+ rows) | `max_steps = 100000` | You will never finish a full epoch. You train until your cloud budget runs out. |
| **Micro-datasets** (<500 rows) | `num_train_epochs = 10` | Small datasets need heavy repetition. Epochs guarantee even exposure across all examples. |
| **Standard Fine-Tuning** (2k - 50k rows) | `num_train_epochs = 3` | The industry standard for instruction-tuning. Guarantees the model sees all diverse data equally without severe overfitting. |
| **Debugging / Testing code** | `max_steps = 50` | When testing if your code crashes, you don't want to wait 4 hours for an epoch to finish. Fast failure. |

### 1.2 The Overfitting Curve

Why don't we just set `num_train_epochs = 100`? If seeing the data once is good, seeing it 100 times must be better, right? No.

Imagine you are studying for a math test. Your teacher gives you a practice exam with 10 questions.
- **Epoch 1:** You solve the questions using the mathematical formulas. You score 80%.
- **Epoch 2:** You study the exact same 10 questions again. You realize question #4 is always "42". You stop using the formula and just memorize "42". You score 90%.
- **Epoch 100:** You have perfectly memorized the answers to all 10 questions without doing any math. You score 100% on the practice exam in record time.

The next day, you take the real exam (validation data). The teacher has changed the numbers in the questions. Because you memorized the answers instead of learning the underlying formulas, you score a 0%.

Neural networks behave identically. If you force a network to read the exact same Sahur conversations 20 times, it will stop learning *how to be sarcastic in general* and will start memorizing *the exact sarcastic jokes from the dataset*. 

### 1.3 The Sweet Spot for LLM Fine-Tuning

Through years of industry trial and error, a consensus has emerged on the optimal number of epochs for fine-tuning Large Language Models.

- **Pre-training (0.5 to 1 Epochs):** When Meta pre-trains Llama on 15 Trillion tokens, they usually only do 1 epoch. The dataset is so massive that by the time they reach the end, the model has already learned language. 
- **Instruction Tuning (1 to 3 Epochs):** When fine-tuning on a curated dataset (like our Sahur personality dataset of 5,000 rows), the sweet spot is almost universally **1 to 3 epochs**.
  - **1 Epoch:** Very safe. Zero risk of overfitting. Great for large instruction datasets.
  - **2-3 Epochs:** The sweet spot for personality datasets. Gives the model enough repetition to thoroughly adapt to the new style without memorizing.
  - **4+ Epochs:** High risk of overfitting. The model will become repetitive.

## 2. Cell-by-Cell Code Breakdown

In professional environments, we don't just blindly guess that Epoch 3 is the correct time to stop. We measure it using a Validation Dataset and an Early Stopping callback.

### 2.1 Validation Split Creation

To detect overfitting, we must hold back some data that the model *never* trains on. We use Hugging Face's `datasets` library to split our data.

```python
# Cell 1: Creating a Validation Split
from datasets import load_dataset

# 1. Load the full dataset
full_dataset = load_dataset("json", data_files="sahur_genz_dataset.jsonl", split="train")

# 2. Split it: 90% for training, 10% for validation (testing)
# The seed ensures reproducibility (we always get the same split)
dataset_dict = full_dataset.train_test_split(test_size=0.1, seed=42)

train_dataset = dataset_dict["train"]
eval_dataset = dataset_dict["test"]

print(f"Training rows: {len(train_dataset)}")
print(f"Validation rows: {len(eval_dataset)}")
```

### 2.2 EarlyStoppingCallback Deep Dive

Now we configure the `SFTTrainer` to evaluate the model on the `eval_dataset` periodically, and automatically abort training if the model starts to overfit.

```python
# Cell 2: Configuring Early Stopping
from transformers import TrainingArguments, EarlyStoppingCallback
from trl import SFTTrainer

# We initialize the EarlyStoppingCallback. 
# early_stopping_patience=3 means: "If the validation loss gets worse (increases) 
# for 3 consecutive evaluations, abort the training run immediately."
early_stop = EarlyStoppingCallback(early_stopping_patience=3)

training_args = TrainingArguments(
    output_dir="./sahur-checkpoints",
    num_train_epochs=5,           # We set a high maximum, trusting Early Stopping to stop it
    per_device_train_batch_size=2,
    
    # Evaluation configuration is REQUIRED for Early Stopping
    eval_strategy="steps",        # Evaluate every X steps
    eval_steps=50,                # Run validation every 50 training steps
    
    # Model Loading (discussed in 7.10)
    load_best_model_at_end=True,
    metric_for_best_model="eval_loss",
    greater_is_better=False,      # For loss, lower is better
)

trainer = SFTTrainer(
    model=model,
    train_dataset=train_dataset,
    eval_dataset=eval_dataset,    # Pass the validation split
    args=training_args,
    callbacks=[early_stop]        # Inject the early stopping mechanism
)
```

### 2.3 Unsloth vs Raw Hugging Face Evaluation

When calculating the evaluation loss, what exactly is the model measuring? 
In Raw Hugging Face, the model calculates Cross-Entropy Loss on *every single token* in the context window, including the user's prompt. 

Unsloth uses `DataCollatorForSeq2Seq` and a feature called `train_on_responses_only()`. This ensures the loss is ONLY calculated on the assistant's generated response, not the user's prompt. This makes the `eval_loss` a much more accurate reflection of the model's actual generative capability.

## 3. Worked Examples / Data Artifacts

Let's look under the hood at the mathematical mechanics of loss masking and evaluation.

### 3.1 CrossEntropyLoss Sequence Masking

How does Unsloth tell the PyTorch loss function to ignore the user's prompt and only calculate loss on the assistant's response? 
It uses the `labels` tensor. In PyTorch's `CrossEntropyLoss`, any token in the `labels` tensor with the value `-100` is completely ignored during the loss calculation and backward pass.

Here is exactly how that masking is constructed manually:

```python
# Cell 3: Manual Sequence Masking (How train_on_responses_only works)
import torch

# Suppose our tokenizer translated this conversation:
# User: "Hi" -> [101, 55]
# Assistant: "Hello" -> [102, 99]
# Padding -> [0, 0]

input_ids = torch.tensor([[101, 55, 102, 99, 0, 0]])

# We clone the input_ids to create the target labels
labels = input_ids.clone()

# MASKING STEP 1: Mask the user prompt (Indices 0 and 1)
labels[0, 0:2] = -100

# MASKING STEP 2: Mask the padding tokens (Indices 4 and 5)
labels[0, 4:6] = -100

print(f"Input IDs: {input_ids.tolist()[0]}")
print(f"Labels:    {labels.tolist()[0]}")
# Output:
# Input IDs: [101, 55, 102, 99, 0, 0]
# Labels:    [-100, -100, 102, 99, -100, -100]
```
When this `labels` tensor is passed to the loss function, the gradients will only flow through tokens `102` and `99` (the assistant's response).

### 3.2 Overfitting Detection Script

If you didn't use `EarlyStoppingCallback`, you can still write a script to analyze a completed training run and find exactly where the model overfit.

```python
# Cell 4: Overfitting Detector
import json

def detect_overfitting(trainer_state_path):
    print("🔎 Analyzing Training Run for Overfitting...\n")
    
    with open(trainer_state_path, "r") as f:
        state = json.load(f)
        
    log_history = state.get("log_history", [])
    
    eval_losses = []
    for log in log_history:
        if "eval_loss" in log:
            eval_losses.append((log["step"], log["eval_loss"]))
            
    if not eval_losses:
        print("No evaluation logs found. Cannot detect overfitting.")
        return
        
    print(f"Found {len(eval_losses)} evaluation points.")
    
    best_loss = float('inf')
    best_step = 0
    consecutive_increases = 0
    
    for step, loss in eval_losses:
        if loss < best_loss:
            best_loss = loss
            best_step = step
            consecutive_increases = 0
        else:
            consecutive_increases += 1
            print(f"⚠️ Warning: Eval loss increased at step {step} (Loss: {loss:.4f})")
            
            if consecutive_increases >= 3:
                print(f"\n🚨 OVERFITTING DETECTED! 🚨")
                print(f"The model began memorizing data after Step {best_step}.")
                print(f"The lowest valid loss was {best_loss:.4f} at Step {best_step}.")
                print("Recommendation: Load the checkpoint from this step.")
                return
                
    print(f"\n✅ Training completed successfully. No severe overfitting detected.")
    print(f"Best model was at Step {best_step} with loss {best_loss:.4f}.")

# detect_overfitting("./sahur-checkpoints/checkpoint-1000/trainer_state.json")
```

## 4. Failure Mode Playbook

> [!WARNING]
> **The Phantom Metric Trap**
> Do not be fooled by a Training Loss that drops beautifully to `0.001`. A training loss approaching zero does not mean your model is smart; it means your model has successfully memorized the dataset. If you do not have an `eval_loss` metric, you are flying completely blind. Always sacrifice 5-10% of your data to the validation split.

### 4.1 Symptom: Underfitting (Stopping Too Early)
- **What it looks like:** Both `train_loss` and `eval_loss` are still decreasing when the run finishes. When you chat with the model, it occasionally adopts the Sahur personality, but often slips back into a boring, generic AI tone.
- **Why it breaks:** You set `num_train_epochs = 1` on a very small dataset (e.g., 500 rows). The model simply didn't see enough examples to permanently shift its internal weight structures.
- **The Fix:** Resume training for 2 more epochs, or increase your learning rate slightly.

### 4.2 Symptom: Overfitting (Stopping Too Late)
- **What it looks like:** The `eval_loss` skyrockets while `train_loss` plummets. When chatting with the model, it hallucinates specific names, variables, or jokes that were in the training dataset, completely ignoring the context of your current question.
- **Why it breaks:** You trained for 10 epochs. The neural network found the "lazy" route and memorized the exact syntax of the training rows. 
- **The Fix:** Use the Overfitting Detection Script to find the `best_step`, load that checkpoint, and discard the rest of the run.

## 5. Mini-Lab / Engineering Challenge: Calculate the Steps

You have a dataset containing exactly **12,000 rows** of instructional dialogue.
You configure your trainer with:
- `per_device_train_batch_size = 4`
- `num_train_epochs = 2`

**Question 1:** How many total forward/backward passes (steps) will the optimizer execute during this entire training run?

You look at the logs and see that your GPU is processing exactly **5 steps per second**.
**Question 2:** How many minutes will the entire training run take to complete?

### Solution Key

**Answer to Question 1:**
First, calculate how many steps make up a single epoch.
$$ \text{Steps per Epoch} = \frac{\text{Dataset Size}}{\text{Batch Size}} $$
$$ \text{Steps per Epoch} = \frac{12000}{4} = 3000 \text{ steps} $$
Since we requested 2 epochs, we multiply the steps per epoch by 2:
$$ \text{Total Steps} = 3000 \times 2 = 6000 \text{ total steps} $$
**Final Answer 1:** The optimizer will execute 6000 steps.

**Answer to Question 2:**
We know the total steps (6000) and the speed (5 steps per second).
$$ \text{Total Seconds} = \frac{\text{Total Steps}}{\text{Speed}} $$
$$ \text{Total Seconds} = \frac{6000}{5} = 1200 \text{ seconds} $$
To convert seconds to minutes, divide by 60:
$$ \text{Total Minutes} = \frac{1200}{60} = 20 \text{ minutes} $$
**Final Answer 2:** The training run will take exactly 20 minutes.
