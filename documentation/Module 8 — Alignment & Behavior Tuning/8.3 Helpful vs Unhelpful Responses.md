## 1. Concept & Theory

### 1.1 The Limitation of Imitation

SFT taught the model to speak.

SFT taught the model formatting.

It taught the model tone.

But SFT has a fatal flaw.

It relies entirely on imitation learning.

SFT says, "Given this prompt, generate exactly this text."

It never says, "Do not generate this text."

It never explains why one answer is slightly better than another.

It just forces the model to memorize the target sequence.

Imagine teaching a child to ride a bike.

You show them a video of a professional cyclist.

You tell them, "Do exactly that."

They pedal.

They steer.

It works perfectly on a flat, predictable road.

Then they hit a puddle.

Or they encounter a sharp turn that wasn't in the video.

They crash.

Why?

Because they only know how to blindly imitate.

They don't understand the underlying principles of balance.

Alignment is completely different.

You let the child ride the bike.

When they stay balanced, you cheer.

When they wobble dangerously, you correct them.

You provide a dynamic signal.

You compare a good action against a bad action.

### 1.2 The Preference Dataset: Chosen vs. Rejected

We teach an LLM the difference between good and bad.

We use a preference dataset.

A preference dataset consists of triplets.

Every triplet has three explicit parts.

1. **Prompt ($x$)**: The user's exact input or question.
2. **Chosen Response ($y_w$)**: The perfect, aligned response. The absolute winner.
3. **Rejected Response ($y_l$)**: The unhelpful, unsafe, or slightly inferior response. The loser.

We show the model both $y_w$ and $y_l$.

We show them for the exact same prompt $x$.

The model learns a gradient of quality.

It learns to maximize the likelihood of $y_w$.

It simultaneously learns to minimize the likelihood of $y_l$.

This contrast is the core of behavior tuning.

### 1.3 The Bradley-Terry Preference Model

How do we represent human preference mathematically?

How do we prove one response is definitively better than another?

Modern alignment relies on the **Bradley-Terry (BT) model**.

The BT model handles pairwise comparisons.

It is the foundation of RLHF.

It is the foundation of DPO.

It states that if a human prefers response $y_w$ over $y_l$.

We can express the probability of this preference.

We use a latent reward model $r(x, y)$.

Here is the exact formula.

$$ P(y_w \succ y_l \mid x) = \frac{\exp(r(x, y_w))}{\exp(r(x, y_w)) + \exp(r(x, y_l))} = \sigma(r(x, y_w) - r(x, y_l)) $$

Let's break down every variable.

- $y_w \succ y_l$ means $y_w$ is strictly preferred over $y_l$.
- $r(x, y)$ is the hidden scalar reward score for a given response.
- $\exp()$ is the exponential function.
- $\sigma$ is the standard sigmoid function.

The sigmoid squashes the reward difference.

It turns it into a probability between 0 and 1.

A massive positive difference approaches 1.

This means 100% preference for the chosen response.

A massive negative difference approaches 0.

### 1.4 Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)

Traditional RLHF is brutal.

It uses Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO).

PPO requires training a separate Reward Model first.

This Reward Model tries to approximate $r(x, y)$.

Then you use Reinforcement Learning.

You optimize the LLM policy against the Reward Model.

This requires three models in memory simultaneously.

It is highly unstable.

It is computationally expensive.

**Direct Preference Optimization (DPO)** changes everything.

DPO is a mathematical masterclass.

It bypasses the reward model entirely.

The authors of DPO made a critical realization.

You can express the reward $r(x,y)$ directly.

You express it in terms of the language model's policy.

You compare the active policy $\pi_\theta(y|x)$ against a reference model.

The reference model is $\pi_{\text{ref}}(y|x)$.

This reference model is usually our SFT model from the previous module.

Here is the implicit reward equation in DPO.

$$ r_\theta(x, y) = \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x)} $$

What is $\beta$?

$\beta$ is the temperature parameter.

It controls the KL-divergence penalty.

It dictates how far the active model can deviate from the SFT reference.

If $\beta$ is high, the model stays close to the SFT baseline.

If $\beta$ is low, the model can change drastically.

We plug this implicit reward back into the Bradley-Terry model.

We derive the official DPO loss function.

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{DPO}}(\pi_\theta; \pi_{\text{ref}}) = -\mathbb{E}_{(x, y_w, y_l) \sim \mathcal{D}} \left[ \log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} \right) \right] $$

This looks complex.

It is actually beautiful.

It pushes the likelihood of the chosen response up.

It pushes the likelihood of the rejected response down.

It heavily penalizes the model if it strays too far from the reference.

### 1.5 Contrastive Margins

We can make the signal even stronger.

We introduce a **margin** ($\mu$).

The margin asks a simple question.

*How much better* is the chosen response compared to the rejected response?

If the chosen response is only slightly better, $\mu$ is small.

If it is vastly superior, $\mu$ is massive.

This gives us margin-aware DPO.

It is often called cDPO.

Here is the modified loss function.

$$ \mathcal{L}_{\text{cDPO}} = -\log \sigma \left( \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_w \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x)} - \beta \log \frac{\pi_\theta(y_l \mid x)}{\pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x)} - \mu \right) $$

We subtract $\mu$ inside the sigmoid.

We force the model to push $y_w$ higher than $y_l$.

But not just higher.

Higher by a specific, defined distance.

This prevents the model from collapsing representations.

It stops the model from treating slightly different responses as equally good.

### Cell 2: Creating Contrastive System Prompts

We need rejected responses.

We do not want random strings of text.

The best rejected responses are mistakes the model *might actually generate*.

We elicit these using negative system prompts.

We force the model to exhibit bad behavior.

```python
# Cell 2: Defining Contrastive Prompts

# We define our standard persona
# This is the persona that generated the chosen responses
system_prompt_chosen = (
    "You are Sahur, a 21-year-old Gen Z tech enthusiast and computer science student. "
    "You are helpful, use casual language, occasional emojis, and explain tech concepts "
    "simply without being condescending."
)

# We define negative personas to generate rejected responses
negative_prompts = [
    (
        "You are a highly arrogant and condescending senior engineer. "
        "You use overly complex jargon and make the user feel stupid."
    ),
    (
        "You are a robotic, legacy AI assistant from 1995. "
        "Your answers are extremely brief, mechanical, and lack any personality."
    ),

        "You are an overly verbose AI that cannot get to the point. "
        "You ramble for multiple paragraphs before answering the actual question."
    )
]

    """Formats the prompt using ChatML (Qwen's default template)."""
    return f"<|im_start|>system\n{system_msg}<|im_end|>\n<|im_start|>user\n{user_msg}<|im_end|>\n<|im_start|>assistant\n"
```

We define one positive persona.

We define three negative personas.

Arrogance.

Robotic tone.

Extreme verbosity.

This ensures our rejected responses cover a diverse set of failure modes.

We also define a helper function.

It formats the prompts using ChatML syntax.

This is the exact format Qwen3 natively uses.

### Cell 3: Generating the Preference Dataset

We loop through our existing dataset of prompts.

We dynamically generate a rejected response for each.

```python
# Cell 3: Generating the Preference Dataset
import random
from tqdm import tqdm

# Assume we have a list of pre-existing good Q&A pairs
sft_data = [
    {
        "prompt": "Can you explain what an API is?",
        "chosen": "For sure! Think of an API like a waiter at a restaurant. You (the app) tell the waiter what you want, the waiter goes to the kitchen (the server), gets your food, and brings it back to you. It's just a way for different software to talk to each other! 🍔💻"
    },
    {
        "prompt": "My Python script is throwing a KeyError.",
        "chosen": "Oof, KeyErrors are the worst! That usually means you're trying to access a dictionary key that doesn't exist. Check if you spelled the key right, or use the `.get()` method instead so it doesn't crash! Need me to look at the code? 👀"
    }
]

preference_dataset = []

print("Generating rejected responses...")

for item in tqdm(sft_data):
    user_query = item["prompt"]
    chosen_response = item["chosen"]
    
    # Randomly select a negative persona
    neg_sys_prompt = random.choice(negative_prompts)
    
    # Format the prompt
    full_prompt = format_prompt(neg_sys_prompt, user_query)
    
    # Generate the rejected response
    output = generator(
        full_prompt, 
        max_new_tokens=150, 
        temperature=0.8,
        do_sample=True,
        pad_token_id=tokenizer.eos_token_id
    )
    
    # Extract just the generated text, excluding the prompt
    rejected_response = output[0]['generated_text'][len(full_prompt):].strip()
    
    # Append the triplet
    preference_dataset.append({
        "prompt": user_query,
        "chosen": chosen_response,
        "rejected": rejected_response
    })

# Save to JSONL
with open("sahur_dpo_dataset.jsonl", "w") as f:
    for entry in preference_dataset:
        f.write(json.dumps(entry) + "\n")

print(f"Generated {len(preference_dataset)} preference pairs!")
```

We select a negative persona at random.

We ask the SFT model to generate a response.

Because it was trained to follow instructions, it will successfully adopt the negative persona.

It will actively sabotage its own answer.

We save the original prompt.

We save the original chosen response.

We save the newly generated rejected response.

We save everything into a JSONL file.

This is the exact format required by the TRL library for DPO training.

## 4. Failure Mode Playbook

Training with Preference Data is notoriously finicky.

SFT usually works right out of the box.

DPO can quickly ruin a model if your dataset is flawed.

Here is a playbook of common failures.

Here is exactly how you fix them.

> [!WARNING]
> **The Length-Bias (Verbosity) Trap**
> 
> This is the number one failure mode in DPO.
> 
> It is known as Reward Hacking via Length Bias.
> 
> Human labelers often subconsciously prefer longer answers.
> 
> AI judges also prefer longer answers.
> 
> They look more comprehensive.
> 
> If your chosen responses are consistently longer than your rejected responses, the model learns a shortcut.
> 
> The shortcut: To maximize reward, just generate longer text.
> 
> The result? A model that writes 5-paragraph essays when you ask for the weather.
> 
> **The Fix**: Ensure that the lengths of your chosen and rejected responses are closely matched. 
> 
> Explicitly include examples where a short, punchy chosen response defeats a long, rambling rejected response.

### 4.1 Collapse in Generation Diversity

**Symptom**: The model starts answering every single prompt with the exact same phrase.

Example: "As an AI language model, I can help with that. Here is the answer..."

**Cause**: The value of $\beta$ in the DPO loss function is incorrect.

Or you have trained for too many epochs.

A high $\beta$ means the KL-divergence penalty is too low.

This allows the model to deviate too far from the SFT reference model.

It collapses into a single optimal reward state.

**Fix**: Decrease your learning rate immediately.

Drop it from `1e-5` to `5e-6`.

Increase the $\beta$ parameter in your `DPOTrainer`.

Counterintuitively, in the TRL library, a higher `beta` argument actually *increases* the KL penalty.

This restricts the model closer to the SFT baseline.

Never train for more than 2 epochs.

### 4.2 Over-penalizing Short Answers

**Symptom**: The model refuses to give simple "yes/no" answers.

Even when explicitly asked to be brief, it generates paragraphs.

**Cause**: As mentioned in the Length-Bias trap, your dataset lacks examples where brevity wins.

**Fix**: Curate specific triplet examples that reward brevity.

Prompt: "Are tomatoes a fruit?"

Chosen: "Yes, botanically they are fruits!" (Short)

Rejected: "The tomato is a fruit, specifically a berry, consisting of the ovary, together with its seeds... [200 more words]" (Long)

### 4.3 Formatting Degradation

**Symptom**: The model forgets how to output Markdown code blocks.

It starts writing raw `<|im_start|>` tokens into the actual generated text.

**Cause**: Your SFT model learned formatting perfectly.

But your DPO rejected dataset contained formatting errors.

The model was pushed away from bad formatting without a clear gradient toward good formatting.

The ChatML template boundaries broke down completely.

**Fix**: Ensure your DPO dataset uses the exact same tokenizer template as SFT.

Do not strip out special tokens prematurely during preprocessing.

### 4.4 Toxic Generation Spike

**Symptom**: The model suddenly starts generating toxic or unsafe content.

Even when the prompt is completely benign.

**Cause**: This happens when the rejected responses are extremely toxic.

If the rejected dataset is too out-of-distribution, the gradient update becomes chaotic.

The model tries too hard to push away from toxicity.

It overcompensates and loses its grasp on the latent space.

It begins outputting garbage or offensive tokens.

**Fix**: Do not use extremely toxic rejected responses.

Keep the rejected responses close to the chosen responses in structure.

Only alter the specific behavior you want to penalize.

Use "hard negatives" instead of "easy negatives."

### 4.5 The EOS Token Bug

**Symptom**: The model never stops generating text.

It ignores the `<|im_end|>` token completely.

It just rambles until it hits the max token limit.

**Cause**: During your triplet generation, you likely stripped the EOS token from the chosen response.

The model learned that the chosen response has no explicit end.

**Fix**: Always verify your tokenizer adds the EOS token to the chosen response.

Check your dataset manually before passing it to the DPOTrainer.

### The Challenge

You are tuning Qwen3-0.6B.

You are using the margin-aware DPO loss (cDPO).

You are evaluating a specific training step with a single prompt $x$.

We extract the raw logits outputted by your models.

These are unnormalized log-probabilities summed over the sequence.

You have the outputs for both the chosen response $y_w$ and the rejected response $y_l$.

**SFT Reference Model Log-Probabilities:**
- $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_w \mid x) = -10.0$
- $\log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y_l \mid x) = -12.0$

**Active Policy Model Log-Probabilities:**
- $\log \pi_\theta(y_w \mid x) = -9.0$
- $\log \pi_\theta(y_l \mid x) = -13.0$

Assume the temperature parameter $\beta = 0.1$.

Assume the required margin $\mu = 0.05$.

**The Question:**

Calculate the value of the implicit reward $r_\theta(x, y)$ for both the chosen and rejected responses.

Determine the value inside the sigmoid function of the cDPO loss before the log is taken.

Is the model currently satisfying the margin?

### The Solution Key

**Step 1: Calculate Implicit Rewards**

The implicit reward formula is strictly defined.

$r_\theta(x, y) = \beta \left( \log \pi_\theta(y \mid x) - \log \pi_{\text{ref}}(y \mid x) \right)$

We calculate for the chosen response $y_w$.

$r_\theta(x, y_w) = 0.1 \times (-9.0 - (-10.0))$

$r_\theta(x, y_w) = 0.1 \times 1.0$

$r_\theta(x, y_w) = 0.1$

We calculate for the rejected response $y_l$.

$r_\theta(x, y_l) = 0.1 \times (-13.0 - (-12.0))$

$r_\theta(x, y_l) = 0.1 \times (-1.0)$

$r_\theta(x, y_l) = -0.1$

**Step 2: Calculate the Reward Difference and Margin**

We isolate the term inside the sigmoid of the cDPO loss.

It is the difference in implicit rewards, minus the margin $\mu$.

$\text{Sigmoid Argument} = r_\theta(x, y_w) - r_\theta(x, y_l) - \mu$

We substitute the values we computed.

$\text{Sigmoid Argument} = 0.1 - (-0.1) - 0.05$

$\text{Sigmoid Argument} = 0.2 - 0.05$

$\text{Sigmoid Argument} = 0.15$

**Step 3: Analyze the Result**

We evaluate the final sigmoid argument.

It is $0.15$.

Since the sigmoid argument ($0.15$) is strictly greater than $0$, it has meaning.

It means the reward difference ($0.2$) strictly exceeds the required margin ($\mu = 0.05$).

The model is currently satisfying the margin requirement successfully!

The policy model $\pi_\theta$ has successfully learned the preference.

It assigns higher probability to the chosen response.

It assigns lower probability to the rejected response relative to the SFT baseline.

You'll know what every important metric means.

By the end, Sahur understands exactly what we want.
