# 8.4 Calibration: Knowing What You Don't Know

## 1. Concept & Theory: The Mechanics of Model Confidence

Our model thinks it knows everything. That's a problem.

How do we teach an AI to say "I don't know"?

We'll extract:
- Confidence scores from logits.
- Uncertainty metrics.
- The boundaries of model knowledge.

You'll calibrate the model to doubt itself when facts are fuzzy.

---

Why does SFT create overconfident models?

During Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT) with Unsloth, we successfully taught Qwen3-0.6B to answer questions. 
However, SFT forces the model to maximize the likelihood of specific target tokens. 
This continuous optimization pushes the logits to extreme, unbounded values. 
The result? The model memorizes training data and loses its nuanced, well-calibrated probabilities.

What is a calibrated LLM?

When an LLM generates a response, it outputs a probability distribution for the next token. 
If the model predicts a token with 90% probability, it must be correct exactly 90% of the time. 
If it predicts a token with 60% probability, it must be correct exactly 60% of the time. 

When this holds perfectly across all confidence levels, we call the model **perfectly calibrated**.

### 1.1 The Meteorologist Analogy

Think of a calibrated LLM like a trusted local meteorologist. 

If they predict a 70% chance of rain, and you track their "70% probability" predictions over 100 days, it must rain on exactly 70 of those days.

- **Overconfident:** It rains 40 times out of 100. They claimed high likelihood, but reality was much lower.
- **Underconfident:** It rains 95 times out of 100. They hedged their bets too much.

Modern post-trained LLMs act like a meteorologist who screams "100% chance of rain!" every single day. They completely ignore uncertainty.

### 1.2 From Logits to Probabilities

How does the model calculate this probability?

We look at the final linear layer (the language modeling head) of our Qwen3-0.6B architecture. 
At the end of the transformer blocks, the model outputs unnormalized, raw scores. We call these **logits** ($z_i$) for each token $i$ in the vocabulary $V$.

To convert these raw logits into a valid probability distribution $p_i$ that sums to 1.0, we apply the Softmax function:

$$
p_i = \frac{\exp(z_i / T)}{\sum_{j=1}^{|V|} \exp(z_j / T)}
$$

Here, $T$ is the **Temperature** scalar.

- **$T=1.0$**: The standard, unmodified softmax function.
- **$T < 1.0$**: The probability distribution becomes sharper. The highest logit dominates even more. The model becomes more confident.
- **$T > 1.0$**: The distribution becomes flatter. Probability mass spreads more evenly. The model becomes artificially less confident.

### 1.3 Sequence-Level vs Token-Level Confidence

You must distinguish between token-level confidence and sequence-level confidence. 

The softmax output gives us the probability of a *single token*. 

The probability of an entire generated sequence $S = (w_1, w_2, ..., w_N)$ given the prompt context $C$ is the product of the individual conditional token probabilities:

$$
P(S | C) = \prod_{i=1}^{N} P(w_i | C, w_{1:i-1})
$$

Probabilities are strictly less than or equal to 1. Therefore, longer sequences have vanishingly small sequence-level probabilities. 
Sequence-level confidence is often evaluated by taking the average log-probability rather than the raw mathematical product. 

Here, we focus exclusively on token-level calibration.

### 1.4 Calibration Metrics: The Brier Score

How do we mathematically measure calibration? 

One historically significant metric is the Brier score. The Brier score measures the accuracy of probabilistic predictions. 

For a single prediction over $C$ distinct classes, it is calculated as the mean squared error between the predicted probabilities $\hat{p}$ and the actual true outcomes $y$. 
Here, $y$ is a one-hot encoded vector representing the correct class.

$$
BS = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=1}^{N} \sum_{c=1}^{C} (\hat{p}_{n,c} - y_{n,c})^2
$$

- If the model assigns 100% probability to the correct token, the Brier score is exactly 0 (perfect score). 
- If it assigns 100% to the wrong token, the score is penalized heavily. 

A lower Brier score always indicates better calibration and predictive accuracy.

### 1.5 Expected Calibration Error (ECE)

The Brier score is rigorous, but **Expected Calibration Error (ECE)** is the modern industry standard for LLMs. ECE is much easier for humans to interpret. 

ECE quantifies the exact gap between a model's predicted confidence and its actual empirical accuracy.

How do we compute ECE?

1. Group all predictions into $M$ uniformly spaced bins based on their confidence scores. 
   - *Example:* 10 bins: 0.0-0.1, 0.1-0.2, ..., 0.9-1.0.
2. For each bin $m$, calculate the average predicted confidence ($\text{conf}(B_m)$) and the true empirical accuracy ($\text{acc}(B_m)$).
3. Take the weighted average of the absolute differences across all $M$ bins.

$$
\text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{M} \frac{|B_m|}{n} \left| \text{acc}(B_m) - \text{conf}(B_m) \right|
$$

Where:
- $n$ is the total number of evaluation predictions.
- $|B_m|$ is the number of predictions in bin $m$.
- $\text{acc}(B_m)$ is the fraction of correct predictions in bin $m$.
- $\text{conf}(B_m)$ is the average predicted probability in bin $m$.

If an LLM is perfectly calibrated, $\text{ECE} = 0$. 

Most fine-tuned SFT models have an ECE between 0.10 and 0.25. They are heavily skewed towards pathological overconfidence.

### Cell 2: Manual Forward Pass for Direct Logit Access

How do we evaluate multiple-choice questions (MCQs) effectively?

Do not use autoregressive generation. 
Autoregressive generation takes time and introduces sampling noise. 

Instead, perform a single, deterministic forward pass. 
Look at the raw logits for the *next* token right after the prompt ends.

```python
# [CELL 2]
def get_choice_probabilities(prompt, choices, model, tokenizer):
    """
    Given a prompt and a list of single-token choices (e.g., [' A', ' B', ' C']),
    returns the normalized probability distribution over just those specific choices.
    """
    # Tokenize the prompt and move tensors to the GPU
    inputs = tokenizer([prompt], return_tensors="pt").to("cuda")
    
    # Tokenize each choice to get its specific vocabulary ID
    choice_tokens = [
        tokenizer.encode(c, add_special_tokens=False)[0] 
        for c in choices
    ]
    
    with torch.no_grad():
        # Perform a single forward pass without calculating gradients
        outputs = model(**inputs)
        
    # Extract logits for the very last token in the input sequence
    # Original Shape: (batch_size, sequence_length, vocab_size) 
    # Sliced Shape: (vocab_size,)
    next_token_logits = outputs.logits[0, -1, :]
    
    # Isolate the logits corresponding to our specific multiple choices
    choice_logits = torch.tensor(
        [next_token_logits[tok] for tok in choice_tokens]
    )
    
    # Apply softmax to get probabilities exclusively over these choices
    choice_probs = torch.softmax(choice_logits, dim=-1)
    
    # Map the probabilities back to the string labels for readability
    results = {
        choice: prob.item() 
        for choice, prob in zip(choices, choice_probs)
    }
    
    return results

# Let's test the function with a dummy prompt
prompt_text = "Question: What is 2+2?\nA) 3\nB) 4\nC) 5\nAnswer:"
probability_distribution = get_choice_probabilities(
    prompt_text, 
    [" A", " B", " C"], 
    model, 
    tokenizer
)

print("Extracted Choice Probabilities:")
print(probability_distribution)
```

What problem does this solve?

By isolating the logits of only the choices we care about (A, B, C) and applying softmax *only* to those logits, we force the model to distribute 100% of its probability mass among the valid answers. 

This is standard practice in LLM evaluation frameworks like EleutherAI's `lm-eval-harness`.

### Cell 3: Extracting Logits during Open-Ended Generation

Sometimes we want the model to generate a long, multi-token response. 
We want to measure its confidence across the entire generated sequence. 

To do this, we must configure Hugging Face's internal `generate()` method to return the scores at every generation step.

```python
# [CELL 3]
def generate_with_confidence(prompt, model, tokenizer):
    inputs = tokenizer([prompt], return_tensors="pt").to("cuda")
    
    outputs = model.generate(
        **inputs,
        max_new_tokens = 20,
        
        # Required to get an output object instead of just tensors
        return_dict_in_generate = True, 
        
        # This flag is mandatory to get raw logits!
        output_scores = True,           
        
        # Use unmodified logits
        temperature = 1.0,              
        
        # Greedy decoding guarantees deterministic reproducibility
        do_sample = False               
    )
    
    # Slice off the input prompt to get just the newly generated tokens
    prompt_length = inputs["input_ids"].shape[1]
    generated_tokens = outputs.sequences[0][prompt_length:]
    
    # outputs.scores is a tuple where each element is the logit tensor 
    # at a specific time step
    token_confidences = []
    
    for i, step_logits in enumerate(outputs.scores):
        # Convert logits to probabilities across the entire vocabulary of 151,936 tokens
        probs = torch.softmax(step_logits[0], dim=-1)
        
        # Look up the probability of the token the model actually chose
        chosen_token_id = generated_tokens[i].item()
        confidence = probs[chosen_token_id].item()
        
        token_confidences.append({
            "token": tokenizer.decode([chosen_token_id]),
            "confidence": confidence
        })
        
    return token_confidences
```

Why does this work?

The `output_scores=True` parameter is the secret. 
It forces the generation loop to cache the logits before it performs the `argmax` operation to select the token. 
We reconstruct the softmax probabilities manually in the loop.

### Cell 4: Calculating Expected Calibration Error (ECE)

Next, we write our core evaluation metric function. 

This function bins the confidences and calculates the expected discrepancy between average confidence and true accuracy.

```python
# [CELL 4]
def compute_ece(confidences, accuracies, num_bins=10):
    """
    Computes ECE for binary correctness (0 or 1).
    """
    confidences = np.array(confidences)
    accuracies = np.array(accuracies)
    
    # Create uniform bins from 0.0 to 1.0
    bins = np.linspace(0, 1, num_bins + 1)
    bin_indices = np.digitize(confidences, bins) - 1
    
    ece = 0.0
    total_samples = len(confidences)
    
    print(f"{'Bin Range':<15} | {'Count':<6} | {'Avg Conf':<8} | {'Avg Acc':<8}")
    print("-" * 45)
    
    for m in range(num_bins):
        # Find all samples that fall into the current bin 'm'
        in_bin = (bin_indices == m)
        bin_samples = np.sum(in_bin)
        
        if bin_samples > 0:
            bin_acc = np.mean(accuracies[in_bin])
            bin_conf = np.mean(confidences[in_bin])
            
            # Print bin statistics for transparency and debugging
            bin_range = f"{bins[m]:.1f} - {bins[m+1]:.1f}"
            print(f"{bin_range:<15} | {bin_samples:<6} | {bin_conf:.4f}   | {bin_acc:.4f}")
            
            # Accumulate the weighted absolute error
            weight = bin_samples / total_samples
            ece += weight * np.abs(bin_acc - bin_conf)
            
    return ece
```

How does this work internally?

We use `np.digitize` to efficiently map each continuous confidence float into a discrete integer bin index. 
The loop calculates the local accuracy and confidence for each bin. 
It then weights the error by how many samples fell into that bin.

### Cell 5: Post-Hoc Calibration via Temperature Scaling

How do we fix miscalibration without undergoing expensive retraining?

The most common, lightweight method is finding an optimal Temperature $T$ on a held-out validation set. We apply this $T$ during inference. 
We'll use SciPy to optimize this parameter.

```python
# [CELL 5]
from scipy.optimize import minimize

def optimize_temperature(logits_list, labels_list):
    """
    Finds the optimal temperature scalar T to minimize Negative Log Likelihood.
    """
    # Shape: (Num_Samples, num_classes)
    logits_tensor = torch.stack(logits_list) 
    
    # Shape: (Num_Samples,)
    labels_tensor = torch.tensor(labels_list) 
    
    def nll_objective(T):
        # Scale the logits by the candidate temperature
        scaled_logits = logits_tensor / T[0]
        
        # Cross entropy computes Negative Log Likelihood mathematically
        loss = torch.nn.functional.cross_entropy(scaled_logits, labels_tensor)
        return loss.item()
    
    # Run the L-BFGS-B optimizer starting with T=1.0
    result = minimize(
        nll_objective, 
        [1.0], 
        bounds=[(0.1, 5.0)], 
        method='L-BFGS-B'
    )
    
    optimal_T = result.x[0]
    
    print(f"Optimization complete. Optimal Temperature: {optimal_T:.3f}")
    return optimal_T
```

What if we change the temperature?

Temperature scaling does not change the argmax prediction. The model's answers remain identical. 
But it rescales the probabilities. 

By minimizing the Negative Log Likelihood (NLL), we pull extreme probabilities toward the center. This directly reduces the ECE.

## 4. Failure Mode Playbook

Evaluating confidence percentages seems mathematically straightforward. 
Applying these metrics to LLM token structures is fraught with edge cases. 

Review these failure modes carefully before building calibration pipelines.

### 4.1 The Tokenizer Sub-word Trap

**The Problem:**
You attempt to extract the confidence of the word "False". 
The tokenizer splits it into two tokens: `["F", "alse"]`. 
Or it includes a leading space, resulting in `[" False"]`. 

**The Fix:**
Always test your tokenizer encodings manually before indexing into your logits tensor. 

```python
# Verify exactly how your specific choices are tokenized
print(tokenizer.tokenize("False")) 

# The leading space fundamentally changes the Token ID!
print(tokenizer.tokenize(" False")) 
```

### 4.2 The Softmax Axis Trap

**The Problem:**
When dealing with batched generations, the logits tensor has the shape `(batch, seq_len, vocab_size)`. 
If you mistakenly apply `torch.softmax(logits, dim=1)` instead of `dim=-1`, you normalize across the sequence length instead of the vocabulary. 
This results in complete gibberish probabilities that sum to 1 across time, not potential classes.

**The Fix:**
Always explicitly specify `dim=-1` when converting logits to probabilities.

> [!WARNING]
> **The `do_sample=True` Logit Masking Trap**
>
> If you evaluate your model using `model.generate(..., do_sample=True, top_k=50)`, Hugging Face modifies the logits *under the hood before* returning them. 
> It sets all logits outside the top 50 to `-infinity`. 
> 
> If you calculate your softmax on these modified logits, your confidence scores will be artificially inflated. 
> The probability mass of the entire long tail of the vocabulary has been completely destroyed!
>
> **The Fix:** For true calibration calculations, use greedy decoding (`do_sample=False`) or extract logits manually using a standard forward pass `model(**inputs)`.

### 4.3 Chat Template Mismatches

**The Problem:**
You trained the model using a specific chat template (like ChatML with `<|im_start|>user`). 
During evaluation, you provide the prompt as raw, unformatted text. 

The model's confidence distribution will instantly collapse. 
It becomes highly uncertain because the text structure is out-of-distribution (OOD) compared to fine-tuning data.

**The Fix:**
Always apply the exact same chat template for inference evaluation. Use what you used during SFT.

### 4.4 Confident Hallucinations and EOS Evasion

**The Problem:**
The model outputs a completely fabricated fact with 99.9% probability. 
It refuses to output the End-Of-Sequence (EOS) token. 
It confidently generates hallucinated paragraphs that ramble incoherently.

**The Fix:**
Monitor the confidence of the EOS token at each step. 
In a well-calibrated model, as a thought organically completes, the probability of the EOS token should gradually spike. 

If the EOS token probability remains uniformly low for hundreds of tokens while producing factual claims, the model is trapped in an overconfident hallucination loop.

Calculate the Expected Calibration Error (ECE) manually using exactly 3 specified bins:

- **Bin 1:** $[0.0, 0.70)$
- **Bin 2:** $[0.70, 0.85)$
- **Bin 3:** $[0.85, 1.0]$

*(Note: The upper bound is exclusive for bins 1 and 2, and inclusive for bin 3).*

---

### Solution Key

We'll break the evaluation data down into our 3 specific bins. 
We'll calculate the metrics for each separately.

**Step 1: Bin 1 $[0.0, 0.70)$ Analysis:**

- Samples falling into this range: 
  - Sample 5: Conf = 0.60, Acc = 0
  - Sample 6: Conf = 0.55, Acc = 1
  - Sample 7: Conf = 0.65, Acc = 0

- Sample Count ($|B_1|$): 3
- Mean Predicted Confidence ($conf(B_1)$): $(0.60 + 0.55 + 0.65) / 3 = 1.80 / 3 = 0.60$
- Mean Empirical Accuracy ($acc(B_1)$): $(0 + 1 + 0) / 3 = 1 / 3 \approx 0.3333$
- Absolute Bin Error: $|0.3333 - 0.60| = 0.2667$

**Step 2: Bin 2 $[0.70, 0.85)$ Analysis:**

- Samples falling into this range: 
  - Sample 8: Conf = 0.75, Acc = 1
  - Sample 9: Conf = 0.72, Acc = 0
  - Sample 10: Conf = 0.78, Acc = 1

- Sample Count ($|B_2|$): 3
- Mean Predicted Confidence ($conf(B_2)$): $(0.75 + 0.72 + 0.78) / 3 = 2.25 / 3 = 0.75$
- Mean Empirical Accuracy ($acc(B_2)$): $(1 + 0 + 1) / 3 = 2 / 3 \approx 0.6667$
- Absolute Bin Error: $|0.6667 - 0.75| = 0.0833$

**Step 3: Bin 3 $[0.85, 1.0]$ Analysis:**

- Samples falling into this range: 
  - Sample 1: Conf = 0.95, Acc = 1
  - Sample 2: Conf = 0.90, Acc = 0
  - Sample 3: Conf = 0.85, Acc = 1
  - Sample 4: Conf = 0.92, Acc = 1

- Sample Count ($|B_3|$): 4
- Mean Predicted Confidence ($conf(B_3)$): $(0.95 + 0.90 + 0.85 + 0.92) / 4 = 3.62 / 4 = 0.905$
- Mean Empirical Accuracy ($acc(B_3)$): $(1 + 0 + 1 + 1) / 4 = 3 / 4 = 0.75$
- Absolute Bin Error: $|0.75 - 0.905| = 0.155$

**Step 4: Final ECE Calculation:**

We weight each bin's absolute error by the proportion of samples it contains (out of the 10 total samples).

$$
\text{ECE} = \sum_{m=1}^{3} \frac{|B_m|}{10} \times \text{Error}_m
$$

$$
\text{ECE} = \left(\frac{3}{10} \times 0.2667\right) + \left(\frac{3}{10} \times 0.0833\right) + \left(\frac{4}{10} \times 0.155\right)
$$

$$
\text{ECE} = (0.3 \times 0.2667) + (0.3 \times 0.0833) + (0.4 \times 0.155)
$$

$$
\text{ECE} = 0.08001 + 0.02499 + 0.0620 = \mathbf{0.1670}
$$

**Final Conclusion:**

The Expected Calibration Error (ECE) for this medical checkpoint is **0.1670 (16.7%)**. 

The data clearly shows the model is highly overconfident across the board. 
For instance, in Bin 3, the model is roughly 90.5% confident on average, but it is only correct 75% of the time.
