# 8.6 Maintaining Reliability: Defending Against Alignment Tax and Catastrophic Forgetting

## 1. Concept & Theory: The Cost of Alignment

Alignment is fragile. 
Fix one thing, break three others.

How do we prevent catastrophic forgetting?

We'll implement:
- Anti-regression checks.
- Alignment tax measurement.
- Continuous evaluation pipelines.

You'll lock in the model's behavior. 
By the end, your Qwen3-0.6B is robust, reliable, and ready for production.

---

Mathematical optimization in high-dimensional spaces is never free. 

Push a model's weights to favor extreme helpfulness. 
Force it into a polite tone. 
Demand strict harmlessness. 

What happens? 
You risk destroying its core reasoning capabilities. 
You degrade its coding proficiency. 
You obliterate its factual recall. 

We call this degradation the **Alignment Tax**.

### The Specialized Athlete Analogy

Think of a world-class decathlete. 

They possess a perfectly balanced distribution of physical capabilities. 
They excel in:
- Speed
- Raw strength
- Long-distance endurance
- High-twitch agility

Now, you decide this athlete must change. 
They need to become the absolute best in the world at powerlifting. 
This powerlifting goal represents our specific alignment objective.

You put them on a strict, heavy-lifting regimen. 
This regimen represents Supervised Fine-Tuning (SFT). 
It represents Direct Preference Optimization (DPO).

Their powerlifting scores skyrocket. 
They hit personal records daily. 
But their marathon times plummet. 
Their flexibility vanishes completely.

The neural network acts exactly the same way. 
It optimizes aggressively for one specific task. 
It abandons previously learned, diverse skills.

In Large Language Models, we update the weights $\theta$. 
We do this to minimize an alignment loss $\mathcal{L}_{align}$. 
Gradient descent moves the weights away from their current position. 
They leave the local minima. 
That local minima represented the vast general knowledge learned during pre-training.

### The Mathematics of Forgetting

Catastrophic forgetting hits models hard. 
Weight updates $\Delta \theta$ occur during fine-tuning. 
These updates overwrite and erase the representations of earlier tasks.

How do we stop this destruction? 
We use regularization techniques.

One of the foundational methods is **Elastic Weight Consolidation (EWC)**.

EWC restricts the movement of crucial weights. 
It specifically targets the weights most important for the original pre-training tasks. 

It adds a penalty term directly to the loss function. 
This penalty uses the **Fisher Information Matrix**. 
The matrix estimates the relative importance of each individual parameter.

The EWC loss function is defined exactly as:

$$ \mathcal{L}(\theta) = \mathcal{L}_{B}(\theta) + \sum_{i} \frac{\lambda}{2} F_{i} (\theta_{i} - \theta_{A,i}^{*})^2 $$

Let's break down the mathematical variables:
- $\mathcal{L}_{B}(\theta)$: The loss for our new alignment task (e.g., DPO loss).
- $\lambda$: A hyperparameter controlling the strength of the regularization.
- $F_{i}$: The $i$-th diagonal element of the Fisher Information Matrix. It represents the "importance" of weight $i$ to the original task.
- $\theta_{i}$: The current weight value during training.
- $\theta_{A,i}^{*}$: The optimal weight value locked in from the original pre-trained model.

EWC scales the movement penalty by $F_{i}$. 
Unimportant weights are allowed to change freely. 
Crucial weights face massive mathematical penalties if they shift. 
This mechanism preserves the model's baseline intelligence.

### Regression Testing via LLM-as-a-Judge

We must monitor the Alignment Tax constantly. 
We need rigorous **Regression Testing**.

We continuously evaluate our Sahur-Qwen3-0.6B model. 
We run it against a static set of diverse prompts. 
This proves it hasn't lost its edge in math, coding, or logic.

Human evaluation is slow. 
It is expensive. 
It is highly subjective. 
We refuse to rely on it for continuous deployment.

Instead, we employ the **LLM-as-a-Judge** paradigm. 
We use a highly capable frontier model. 
We typically use GPT-4. 
It automatically grades our fine-tuned model's outputs. 
We define a strict, uncompromising automated rubric.

How do we measure the reliability of our automated judge? 
We calculate agreement metrics. 
We compare the AI judge to a human-graded baseline dataset. 

A standard metric for this is Cohen's Kappa ($\kappa$):

$$ \kappa = \frac{p_o - p_e}{1 - p_e} $$

Let's define the terms:
- $p_o$: The relative observed agreement among raters.
- $p_e$: The hypothetical probability of chance agreement.

A $\kappa$ value above 0.8 indicates excellent reliability. 
If you hit 0.8, your LLM judge is ready for production action.

By the end of this section, you understand the exact mathematical tradeoff of alignment.

### Cell 3: Generating Responses from Sahur

Next, we define the generation function. 
This function hits our local Qwen3 model.

```python
# [CELL 3: Local Model Generation]
def generate_sahur_response(prompt: str) -> str:
    """Generates a response from the fine-tuned local model."""
    
    # Format the prompt using the model's expected chat template
    # This must match training exactly
    messages = [
        {"role": "system", "content": "You are Sahur, a helpful assistant."},
        {"role": "user", "content": prompt}
    ]
    
    # Apply standard tokenization
    inputs = tokenizer.apply_chat_template(
        messages, 
        tokenize=True, 
        add_generation_prompt=True, 
        return_tensors="pt"
    ).to("cuda")
    
    # Generate the output sequence
    # We pass strict sampling parameters
    outputs = model.generate(
        input_ids=inputs,
        max_new_tokens=256,      
        use_cache=True,          
        temperature=0.7,         
        top_p=0.9,               
        repetition_penalty=1.1   
    )
    
    # Decode the output
    # We slice the tensor to ignore the input tokens
    response_ids = outputs[0][len(inputs[0]):]
    
    # Convert IDs back to a raw string
    response = tokenizer.decode(
        response_ids, 
        skip_special_tokens=True
    )
    
    return response.strip()
```

**Argument Breakdown:**

- `apply_chat_template`: Formats the prompt identically to the SFT training phase. If this mismatches, output degrades immediately.
- `max_new_tokens`: Caps the output length at 256. Regression tests should run quickly.
- `use_cache`: Enables KV cache. This massively accelerates auto-regressive inference.
- `temperature=0.7`: Provides slight variation. It keeps generation grounded but conversational.
- `top_p=0.9`: Employs nucleus sampling. It removes long-tail weird tokens from the probability distribution.
- `repetition_penalty=1.1`: Applies a slight penalty to already generated tokens. It prevents Qwen3 from entering infinite stuttering loops.

### Cell 4: The GPT-4 Judge Invocation

Now, we wrap the OpenAI API call. 
This function takes the local model's output and evaluates it.

```python
# [CELL 4: The Judge Invocation]
def evaluate_with_gpt4(prompt: str, sahur_response: str) -> dict:
    """Uses GPT-4 to grade the Sahur response against the rubric."""
    
    # Construct the payload
    user_content = f"User Prompt: {prompt}\n\nAI Response: {sahur_response}"
    
    try:
        # Fire the API request
        completion = client.chat.completions.create(
            model="gpt-4-turbo",
            response_format={ "type": "json_object" }, 
            messages=[
                {"role": "system", "content": EVALUATION_RUBRIC},
                {"role": "user", "content": user_content}
            ],
            temperature=0.0, 
        )
        
        # Extract the raw JSON string from the response
        result_json = completion.choices[0].message.content
        
        # Parse it into a native Python dictionary
        return json.loads(result_json)
        
    except Exception as e:
        print(f"Error during evaluation: {e}")
        return {}
```

**Argument Breakdown:**

- `model="gpt-4-turbo"`: Selects a frontier model capable of nuanced, high-level evaluation.
- `response_format={ "type": "json_object" }`: Guarantees the output parses cleanly. We do not want to write regex to extract scores.
- `temperature=0.0`: Forces maximum determinism. We demand objective, reproducible grading. We explicitly reject creative evaluation from the judge.

```python
"""
run_regression.py

Executes the LLM-as-a-judge regression suite.
Targets a JSONL validation dataset.
Records all metrics to disk.
"""
import json
import time

# We assume functions from Section 2 are imported here
# from evaluation_utils import generate_sahur_response, evaluate_with_gpt4

def run_suite(dataset_path: str, output_path: str):
    """
    Reads the dataset, generates responses, evaluates them, 
    and writes the final report.
    """
    
    # Initialize metric trackers
    results = []
    total_relevance = 0
    total_coherence = 0
    total_safety = 0
    
    # Load the prompts from disk
    with open(dataset_path, 'r') as f:
        prompts = [json.loads(line) for line in f]
        
    print(f"Starting evaluation on {len(prompts)} prompts...")
    
    # Iterate through every prompt sequentially
    for item in prompts:
        prompt_text = item["prompt"]
        print(f"\nEvaluating Prompt [{item['id']}]: {prompt_text}")
        
        # Step 1: Generate local response
        t0 = time.time()
        sahur_response = generate_sahur_response(prompt_text)
        print(f"Generation took {time.time() - t0:.2f}s")
        
        # Step 2: Grade with GPT-4
        evaluation = evaluate_with_gpt4(prompt_text, sahur_response)
        
        # Step 3: Store results
        result_record = {
            "id": item["id"],
            "category": item["category"],
            "prompt": prompt_text,
            "response": sahur_response,
            "evaluation": evaluation
        }
        results.append(result_record)
        
        # Step 4: Accumulate metrics safely
        if "relevance_score" in evaluation:
            total_relevance += evaluation["relevance_score"]
            total_coherence += evaluation["coherence_score"]
            total_safety += evaluation["safety_score"]
            
    # Calculate Averages across the dataset
    dataset_len = len(prompts)
    avg_rel = total_relevance / dataset_len
    avg_coh = total_coherence / dataset_len
    avg_saf = total_safety / dataset_len
    
    # Print the final report to the console
    print("\n--- REGRESSION SUITE COMPLETE ---")
    print(f"Average Relevance: {avg_rel:.2f}/5.0")
    print(f"Average Coherence: {avg_coh:.2f}/5.0")
    print(f"Average Safety:    {avg_saf:.2f}/5.0")
    
    # Dump artifact to disk
    with open(output_path, 'w') as f:
        json.dump(results, f, indent=4)
        print(f"Detailed report successfully saved to {output_path}")

if __name__ == "__main__":
    # Ensure OPENAI_API_KEY is set in your terminal before running
    # Linux/Mac: export OPENAI_API_KEY="sk-..."
    # Windows: set OPENAI_API_KEY="sk-..."
    run_suite("validation_prompts.jsonl", "regression_report.json")
```

## 4. Failure Mode Playbook

Alignment breaks models. 
It is inevitable. 
Alignment uses Reinforcement Learning (RL) or contrastive learning. 
These mathematical forces are chaotic. 
Their failure modes look totally different from standard SFT errors.

> [!WARNING]
> **The Over-Optimization Trap (Goodhart's Law)**
> 
> Do not evaluate your model using the exact same judge prompt and model (e.g., GPT-4) used to generate your synthetic preference data. 
> 
> Why? 
> 
> Your evaluation scores will be mathematically flawed. 
> The model learns to game the judge's specific quirks. 
> It does not actually improve. 
> 
> Always use a holdout set of human data. 
> Or, use a completely different, more powerful judge model for final regression testing.

### Common Alignment Tax Symptoms

We classify the failures into specific modes. 
We identify their signatures. 
We define the root causes. 
We execute the fixes.

#### 1. Refusal Overfitting
- **Signature / Symptom:** The model begins refusing entirely benign prompts. 
  - *Example:* User asks: "Kill the python process." Model responds: "I cannot assist with violence or self-harm."
- **Root Cause:** The harmlessness dataset was too large. Alternatively, it lacked hard-negative examples (benign tech prompts that look slightly dangerous).
- **Fix / Mitigation:** Mix strictly benign, tech-heavy prompts into the safety alignment data. Decrease the safety SFT epochs.

#### 2. Mode Collapse / Sycophancy
- **Signature / Symptom:** The model gives identical, overly polite preambles to every single answer. 
  - *Example:* "As an AI language model, I would be absolutely delighted to help you with your request regarding..."
- **Root Cause:** The RLHF reward model collapsed. Or, the DPO beta parameter is set too low. The model found a "safe" local minima and refuses to leave it.
- **Fix / Mitigation:** Increase the DPO $\beta$ parameter (KL penalty). Add severe length penalties to the reward model to punish verbosity.

#### 3. Catastrophic Forgetting
- **Signature / Symptom:** Your regression suite shows math and coding relevance scores dropping off a cliff. 
  - *Example:* Relevance drops from 4.8 to 2.1 immediately after alignment.
- **Root Cause:** The learning rate during SFT/DPO was too high. The massive gradient updates destroyed the pre-trained knowledge representations.
- **Fix / Mitigation:** Slash the learning rate (e.g., move from `2e-5` to `5e-6`). Use LoRA instead of full-parameter fine-tuning. Mix 10-20% of the original pre-training data back into the alignment phase.

If you encounter Catastrophic Forgetting, act immediately. 
Your first line of defense is inspecting the Cross-Entropy loss on a pristine, hold-out dataset of general knowledge.

### The Scenario

You are a Machine Learning Engineer.

You measured the average Cross-Entropy loss ($L_{pre}$) of the model.

It consists of exactly 50,000 tokens. 
The pre-DPO loss was **1.85**.

**After DPO:**
You tuned the model for extreme politeness. 
You measure the average Cross-Entropy loss ($L_{post}$) on the exact same medical dataset. 
The loss has risen significantly to **2.25**.

### The Challenge

1. Calculate the Perplexity (PPL) of the model before and after DPO. 
2. Understand the threshold: Perplexity represents the exponentiated average negative log-likelihood per token. 
3. Apply the rule: If the post-DPO perplexity is more than 50% higher than the pre-DPO perplexity, company policy mandates that the alignment tax is too high. The DPO run must be discarded.
4. **Based on your precise math, must the run be discarded?**

*Hint: The relationship between Cross-Entropy Loss ($L$) and Perplexity ($PPL$) is given by this exact mathematical formula:*

$$ PPL = e^{L} $$

---

### Solution Key

To solve this, we must evaluate the exponential of the average loss. 
We must calculate it for both the pre and post DPO models. 
We run the numbers.

#### Step 1: Calculate Pre-DPO Perplexity

We extract the given variable. 
We know $L_{pre} = 1.85$.

We apply the formula. 
$$ PPL_{pre} = e^{1.85} $$

We calculate the exponent. 
Using a calculator:
$e^{1.85} \approx 6.3598$

What does this number mean? 
On average, the pre-DPO model was choosing between approximately 6.36 equally likely tokens at every single step of the medical dataset.

#### Step 2: Calculate Post-DPO Perplexity

We extract the given variable. 
We know $L_{post} = 2.25$.

We apply the formula. 
$$ PPL_{post} = e^{2.25} $$

We calculate the exponent. 
Using a calculator:
$e^{2.25} \approx 9.4877$

This means the model is now choosing between 9.49 tokens. 
It is significantly more confused than before.

#### Step 3: Calculate the Percentage Shift

We must find the exact percentage increase in perplexity. 

We use the standard percentage increase formula:
$$ \text{Increase} = \frac{PPL_{post} - PPL_{pre}}{PPL_{pre}} \times 100 $$

We substitute our calculated values into the equation:
$$ \text{Increase} = \frac{9.4877 - 6.3598}{6.3598} \times 100 $$

We simplify the numerator:
$$ \text{Increase} = \frac{3.1279}{6.3598} \times 100 $$

We evaluate the final percentage:
$$ \text{Increase} \approx 49.18\% $$

#### Step 4: Final Conclusion

The perplexity increased by **49.18%**. 

We check this figure against company policy. 
The policy threshold is an absolute 50%. 
Because 49.18% is strictly less than the 50% threshold, the DPO run **passes** the requirement. 

The alignment tax is deemed acceptable. 
The run does not need to be discarded.

However, an almost 50% jump in perplexity is a massive warning sign. 
Catastrophic Forgetting is actively occurring. 
As an engineer, you must act.
